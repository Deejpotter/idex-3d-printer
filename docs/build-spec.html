Cheap Fast IDEX

A dual-extrusion printer, direct drive, as cheap as possible and as fast as the frame allows — built on the layout you already drew. This version explains the reasoning rather than just stating it, so you can argue with the conclusions.

Lesson 1

A printer is a weight on the end of a spring

Pin a ruler to the edge of a desk, flick the free end, and it wobbles at one particular rate. Not a random rate — one specific one. Make the ruler stick out further and the wobble slows down. Stick a lump of Blu-Tack on the end and it slows down again.

Your print head is that ruler. It hangs off a belt, which hangs off brackets, which bolt to a frame. All of that flexes a little. So when a motor yanks the head sideways and then stops, the head keeps wobbling for a moment. That wobble prints as ringing — the faint repeated echo you see just after a sharp corner.

The rate it wobbles at is the natural frequency, measured in hertz (Hz, wobbles per second). It depends on exactly two things:

natural frequency = (1 ÷ 2π) × √( stiffness ÷ mass )

Stiffness is how hard the structure resists being pushed out of shape, in newtons per metre (N/m) — push with so many newtons, it deflects so many metres. A high number is a rigid thing. Mass is simply the weight that's moving, in kilograms.

In plain terms

Stiffer frame → faster wobble → better. Heavier head → slower wobble → worse. Those are your only two levers, and the square root means you need to quadruple stiffness, or quarter the mass, to double the frequency.

Why do we want a fast wobble? Because a fast wobble dies out quickly and is easier for the firmware to cancel. That's the next two lessons.

Lesson 2

In a chain of springs, the floppiest one wins

This is the idea that decides your whole build, so it's worth getting properly.

The head isn't connected to the motor by one spring. It's a chain: the belt stretches a bit, the bracket holding the motor flexes a bit, the frame those bolt to bends a bit. Springs in a chain like this are called springs in series.

Here's the counterintuitive bit. If you hook a very stiff spring to a very floppy one, the pair is floppier than the floppy one alone. Pull on the chain and the floppy one does nearly all the stretching. Making the stiff one even stiffer changes almost nothing.

This is why people's intuition about printers is usually wrong. Everyone obsesses over belt tension and carriage weight. The actual floppy link is nearly always the printed plastic bracket holding the motor or the idler pulley.

How we know the model is right

If you calculate using the belt alone, you predict the X axis wobbles at 123 Hz. Real bedslingers measure 32–45 Hz. The gap is the brackets. Once they're included, the model lands on 32.5 Hz for a printed-bracket machine — which is what an Ender 3 actually measures. That's the check that says these numbers can be trusted.

Lesson 3

What the wobble costs you in speed

Modern firmware can cancel ringing. The trick is called input shaping: the firmware knows your wobble frequency, so when it commands a sharp move it splits it into two nudges, timed so the second nudge's wobble cancels the first one's. Like catching a swing at exactly the right moment to stop it dead.

It works brilliantly, but it has a price. The two nudges are spread over time, which slightly smears sharp corners. The faster you accelerate, the more smearing. And the lower your wobble frequency, the further apart the nudges must be, so the worse the smearing at any given acceleration.

That gives a practical ceiling on acceleration, roughly:

max acceleration (mm/s²) ≈ frequency² × 0.022

The square is what matters. Going from 30 Hz to 60 Hz doesn't double your acceleration — it quadruples it.

Lesson 6

Motor torque, and why you don't need a NEMA 23

Torque is twisting force, measured in newton-metres (Nm). One newton-metre is a one-newton push on the end of a one-metre lever — roughly the force of holding up a 100 g apple, a metre out.

A belt motor's lever is the radius of its pulley. Your GT2 20-tooth pulley has a working radius of only 6.366 mm, so the motor has a very short lever. That sounds bad but it's the opposite: a short lever means a small torque produces a large pull on the belt. It's gearing in your favour.

torque needed = mass × acceleration × pulley radius (+ a bit to spin the motor's own rotor)

Work it through for the heaviest demand on the machine and the answer is almost embarrassing:

"NEMA 17" is just a mounting standard — it means a 42 × 42 mm faceplate, nothing about power. NEMA 23 means 57 × 57 mm.

The hardest-working axis on the whole printer is Z, lifting the gantry on two lead screws, and it asks for 0.175 Nm. A NEMA 23 delivers 1.0–2.0 Nm. That's six to eleven times more than the worst case anywhere on the machine, for an extra ~700 g of motor and about 2.8 amps — which is more current than a TMC2209 driver chip can give, so you'd have to buy external drivers as well. On a build where the goal is cheap, it's spending money to gain nothing.

Answer to your question

No NEMA 23. Keep the single NEMA 17 belt-linking both lead screws, exactly as you have it — it has 2.6× margin, which is healthy. You own five of the six motors you need; buy one standard NEMA 17.

Lesson 7

Why motors go weak when they spin fast

A spinning motor acts as a generator, pushing back a voltage of its own against the supply. This is back-EMF (electromotive force — just an old word for voltage). The faster it spins, the harder it pushes back.

Your power supply has to overcome that back-voltage before any current can flow into the coils, and current is what makes torque. When back-EMF approaches your supply voltage, current stops flowing and the motor goes limp — no matter how beefy it is.

Voltage left over after back-EMF ("headroom")

A negative number means the motor is generating more voltage than you're feeding it — it simply cannot go that fast. On 12 V you run out at about 250 mm/s. On 24 V you reach roughly 400 mm/s, which is past what the frame can use anyway.

Decision

24 V. It is the cheapest single thing you can do for speed. 36 V would go further but costs more and narrows your board options for no benefit you could actually use.

Lesson 8

What IDEX actually is, and what it costs

IDEX stands for Independent Dual EXtruder. Two complete print heads sit on the same beam, each with its own motor and its own belt, so either can move while the other sits parked at the end.

Compare that to the E3D Chimera in your current design, which is one block with two nozzles side by side, both always moving together. The idle nozzle drags over your print and dribbles on it. IDEX parks the idle nozzle off to the side instead, which is the main reason it gives cleaner dual-colour results.

IDEX also unlocks duplicate mode: both heads print the same object at the same time, two copies in the time of one. That's a genuine throughput win rather than a convenience.

The cost is beam length. Both heads need to reach the whole bed, and both need somewhere to park clear of it:

MeasurementValueNote
Bed width235 mmyour existing bed

MGN12 rail length450 → buy 500 mmyour longest is 350 mm
X beam length~600 mmplus both motor mounts
Build volume235 × 235 × 250single head
Duplicate mode85 mm per copytwo at once

Your 390 mm beam is simply too short for this. A ~600 mm length of 2040 or 2060 fixes it.

Why direct drive was the right call

Direct drive means the motor that pushes filament sits on the moving head. Bowden means it sits on the frame and pushes filament down a long tube. Bowden gives a lighter head, but the filament squashes inside that tube like a spring, so when you command a retraction the nozzle responds late and vaguely.

For dual extrusion that matters more than usual, because precise retraction is exactly what stops the parked nozzle oozing. You chose direct drive, and Lesson 4 shows the mass penalty barely costs you anything once the brackets are aluminium. It's the right call.

Lesson 9

Making the brackets: mill them, or buy them?

Before the cost question, something that changes what "a stiff bracket" even means.

Take a 3 mm aluminium plate, 70 mm wide, sticking out 40 mm. Push on it two different ways:

How the load hits itStiffness
Across the face — bending it like a diving board509 kN/m
In the plate's own plane — pushing along it edge-on500× stiffer

Same plate, same metal, same bolts. The only difference is which way the force arrives. Think of a sheet of paper: floppy flat, but almost impossible to squash edge-on.

So "aluminium bracket" was shorthand

What Lesson 4 really wants is a bracket that isn't loaded in bending. A cheap 3 mm bought plate, turned the right way, beats a thick milled block turned the wrong way. Orientation matters more than material or thickness.

Design every motor and idler mount so the belt pulls along the plate, not across it. Standard V-slot motor plates are already designed this way — the motor bolts to the face and the belt runs in the plate's plane. Use at least two bolts into the extrusion, with proper T-nuts; past that point the bolted joint is the limit, not the plate, which lands you around the 150 kN/m "aluminium" column.

Lesson 10

How it all bolts together — and where I had this wrong

You pushed back on this and you were right. I went back into the assembly and read it properly this time: every occurrence, every transform, every fastener position. Several things I told you earlier were wrong, and the correction changes the whole recommendation.

Corrections
  • The assembly has 294 occurrences, not 77. (77 is just the Frame's child count — I quoted a sub-total as the whole.)
  • It has 153 fasteners, not 140.
  • It records 2 joints and 4 rigid groups across all 294 occurrences, with 0 user parameters and a 539-item timeline. Position is held by captured snapshots, not constraints.
  • And the big one: the upright feet are already reinforced. I said they weren't.

What the feet actually do

Each upright is a 40 × 40 V-slot sitting on a 40 × 40 base rail, and each foot carries:

  • 4 × M5 blind screws up through the base rail's top wall into the four tapped bores of the upright — a 2 × 2 pattern at ±10 mm, which is exactly the 4040's four cell centres. All four are used.
  • 2 × 90° joining plates on the outer face, 4 mm thick, 10 M5 screws between them. All four plates in the design are at the upright feet — not "all up in the Z assembly" as I claimed.

Why blind joining is the strong option — your instinct was correct

A bolted butt joint is not a hinge with a spring in it. Once the bolts are preloaded, the two end faces are clamped together, and the joint behaves as effectively rigid right up until the moment that tries to pull the faces apart exceeds the clamping force. That is called gapping, and until it happens the joint is as stiff as solid metal.

Four M5 bolts at ±10 mm, preloaded to even a conservative 2.5 kN each, resist gapping up to roughly 130 N·m. The moment this machine actually applies at an upright foot — the toolhead at 1.42 kg accelerating hard, 350 mm up — is about 3 N·m. You are a factor of forty inside the limit. The joint never gaps, never slips, and never becomes the floppy spring I modelled it as.

My earlier figure of 91 kN/m effectively modelled a single centre bolt. That is the case where blind joining is genuinely weak, and it is where the "always use corner brackets" folklore comes from. With all four bores used, it does not apply.

Two things that are genuinely wrong at the feet

The joint type is right. The detailing is not.

  • Every blind screw in the model is an M5 × 8. Through a 2.2 mm wall that leaves 5.8 mm of thread in aluminium — below the rule of thumb of two diameters, and below the preload the joint needs to stay clamped. Preload is the entire reason a blind joint is stiff, so this is the one detail that could turn your good joint into my bad one. Use M5 × 16 at the feet. It is the cheapest fix in this document.
  • Both gussets are on the same face. They sit on the outer X face of each upright, so they resist the upright tipping in Y. What the model does not fasten at all

The Frame (64 fasteners), X axis (43) and Z axis (40) are reasonably detailed. Two areas are not, so when you rebuild you will be designing these joints rather than copying them:

  • The whole Y axis — zero fasteners. Not one screw in the entire sub-assembly: nothing holding the Y motor mount, the pulley bracket, the bed support plate, the MGN blocks or the bed-levelling hardware. Roughly 20 missing.
  • The toolhead — four. Two M3 screws and two M3 nuts, inside one Titan. The carriage plate, both NEMA 17s and both extruder bodies are floating. Another 20 or so.
  • The Z top motor has three M4 screws, where a NEMA 17 has four mounting holes.

Making it easier to assemble

  • Drop-in T-nuts everywhere. Never sliding ones. The single biggest assembly-ease win. A sliding T-nut has to be threaded in from the open end before you close that extrusion off — forget one and you dismantle the frame to add it. You already use 32 ball-spring nuts; replace the 10 sliding ones too.
  • Buy M5 in 8, 12, 16 and 20 mm. M5 × 8 is right for a T-nut under a 3 mm plate and wrong for almost everything else. The feet need 16.
  • Keep a ball-end hex driver. The blind screws go in down a long clearance hole inside the base rail's slot, at an angle a straight key will not reach.
  • Build the X beam as a complete unit on the bench — rail, both carriages, both motors, idlers, belts — then lift it onto the Z carriages as one piece.
  • Count your T-nuts into each extrusion before you close a corner. Write the number on masking tape. Add two spares per slot.
  • Torque the blind screws properly, in a cross pattern, like a wheel. Preload is what makes this joint work; an under-torqued blind bolt really is the floppy hinge I wrongly described.

Step 5 takes an hour. You stick an accelerometer on the head, the firmware shakes the axis and tells you its frequency. If it comes back well below the aluminium column, something is flexing that shouldn't be — and finding that with one carriage on the beam is far easier than with two.

Three things that bite IDEX builds

  • The two carriages won't match. They sit at different distances from their motors, so their belts are different lengths and they ring at different frequencies. The firmware lets you set a different correction per carriage for X, but the Y setting must be identical for both. Keep the two belt paths as close to mirror images as you can.
  • The parked nozzle still oozes, just away from your part. You'll want a prime tower — a small sacrificial pillar printed alongside, where each nozzle wipes itself clean before returning.
  • Two nozzles means two sets of offsets. You probe with one head, then measure how far the second sits from it in X, Y and Z. Set aside an evening; this is the part that tests patience.

The numbers that drive it

You set the build volume; the frame sizes itself. The one worth understanding is the X beam, because IDEX is what makes it long:

x_beam_len = build_x + 2 × carriage_w + 2 × park_gap + 2 × end_allow

That is the build area, plus a whole carriage parked clear at each end, plus the gap that keeps a parked nozzle off your print, plus room for a motor and an idler. At 230 mm build it comes to 480 mm — not the ~600 mm I guessed earlier. A 500 mm MGN12 rail fits it exactly.

This is the real cost of IDEX, and now you can see it move: widen a carriage from 60 to 75 mm and the beam grows to 510 mm and the whole frame with it. The other drivers are build_y (the frame is 2 × build_y + 60 deep, because a bedslinger needs to swing the bed its full depth), upright_h, and p40 / p20 for the profile faces.

Lesson 14

Making it real: STEP parts, and the mass error that hid in plain sight

The frame model is saved and parametric. The next job is replacing the remaining blocks with real parts — and that turned up a mistake worth leading with.

Correction — the frame is 4.5 kg, not 13 kg

Every component in the new model inherited Fusion's default material, Steel. The geometry was right; the density was 7,850 kg/m³ instead of 2,700. That inflated the frame by a factor of 2.9 — the 12.99 kg I quoted was really 4.48 kg.

It did not touch any acceleration figure, because those are driven by the toolhead mass, which came from the old assembly. But it would have poisoned every mass number from here on. It is now Aluminum 6061 throughout, and it checks out: the 2020 reads 0.463 kg/m against OpenBuilds' published 0.47.

The layout skeleton weighed 144 kg

The hidden layout bodies — the parametric jig the real parts are jointed to — were steel too, and being blocks rather than profiles they came to 143.8 kg. Any mass query on the assembly was meaningless.

A jig should not weigh anything, so it now carries a one-off material, LAYOUT – massless, at 1 kg/m³. The whole skeleton is 18 g: visible to joints, invisible to the scales. This is the cheap version of the right answer — the pure fix is parameter-driven construction planes with joint origins on them, which carry no mass at all, but that is a rebuild of all ten joints for 18 g.

Seven rules I am building the rest to

  1. Use STEP for shape, never for mass. A stepper motor STEP file is a block with a shaft. It has no iron, copper or magnet inside, so its computed mass is pure fiction. Import the shape, then override the component mass with the datasheet figure. Same for the hotend and the heated bed. This is free and it is the single biggest accuracy gain available.
  2. Link parts, don't paste them. Insert from the hub as a linked external component. One update fixes every instance, the timeline stays short, and the file stays small.
  3. Watch the triangle count. The E3D V6 STEP in your Drive is 51 MB. A hotend at full fidelity will make the assembly crawl. Use it once to check clearances, then substitute a simplified body with the real mass.
  4. STEP has no parameters — so cut it. A STEP rail is one fixed length. Insert it and add a parametric cut at the end, which is what happens in reality anyway. Don't re-trace it the way I did for the extrusions; that was only necessary because no 480 mm part existed.
  5. Don't model 153 fasteners as solids. They contribute nothing to stiffness and bloat everything. Model one of each type, properly, so the bill of materials is real and screw length is checkable — which is exactly how the M5 × 8 defect in Lesson 10 was found — and leave the rest as holes.
  6. Re-joint part-to-part once positions settle. Everything currently hangs off the layout. That is right for a skeleton, but it means the model cannot tell you whether two parts actually touch. Joint the real parts to each other and interference checks start meaning something.
  7. Appearances last, but do them. Anodised grey for extrusion, raw aluminium for plates, steel for rails and fasteners. Five minutes, and it is the difference between a screenshot you can read and a black silhouette.

Lesson 16

Z axis and toolheads — and what modelling them changed

The Z axis and both toolheads are now in the model. Three things came out of it that no amount of arithmetic on paper would have caught.

The nozzle was 68 mm inside the bed

The gantry height had been a placeholder: gantry_z = base_h + 60 mm, set before any toolhead existed. The moment a real toolhead was modelled — rail block, plate, extruder, hotend — the nozzle landed at z 25 with the bed surface at z 93.

It is now gantry_z = bed_top + th_drop, where th_drop (75 mm) is the beam underside to the nozzle tip. The gantry height is an output of the toolhead, not a choice. This is the clearest single argument for modelling before buying.

Where the two carriage blocks go, settled properly

Lesson 10 said "two MGN12H blocks". It did not say where, and the two options are not equivalent.

The toolhead's mass sits about 40 mm forward of the rail. Accelerating along X at that offset makes a moment about Z. Two blocks separated along X resist it with a force couple. Two blocks separated along Z — one rail above the other on the beam face — cannot make a couple about Z at all, so they do nothing for the dominant mode, however appealing the arrangement looks.

So the blocks go in line along X. The catch is that the carriage grows to 100 mm and the beam grows with it, and beam stiffness falls as 1/L³:

Choosing the arrangement — at the 1.14 kg toolhead used for the decision
ArrangementBeamk totalAccelGain
1 block48089.84,411—
2 blocks in line520122.66,021+37%

The bed is now the slowest axis

The built toolhead models out at 518 g — lighter than the 900 g target, because the Orbiter and a V6 on a 6 mm plate is simply a small thing. Running the chain with the measured mass:

Motor torque is not the constraint: 0.45 N·m derated to 60% through a 20T pulley is 42 N, which is 82,000 mm/s² on the toolhead and 23,000 on the bed. The structure binds first in both cases.

A clash the interference check found

The Z carriage block and the lead screw overlapped by 4.5 mm in X. Moving the screws inboard to x 60 and 460 and widening the Z plates to 80 mm cleared it. Interference now reports four overlaps of exactly 4320 mm³ — which is 45 × 12 × 8, the simplified carriage blocks passing through the rails they would really wrap. That is the intended simplification; any other number would be a fault.

  • Simplified with a mass override: carriage blocks (55 g), Orbiter extruders (148 g), V6 hotends (100 g), 5015 fans (35 g). Interference: 26 found, 15 real, 11 by design

Completing the machine threw up 26 overlaps. Fifteen were genuine mistakes and are fixed:

  • Both X motor pulleys fouled their own mount plates — the pulley sat where the plate was. Pulleys moved out; plates given a Ø23 clearance bore for the motor's pilot boss.
  • Both X idlers and the Y idler fouled their brackets. Brackets moved back.
  • The Z screw bearing blocks had no bore at all — solid blocks with a lead screw through them.

The remaining eleven are all parts that would carry a bore in reality: carriage blocks wrapping rails (4 × 4,320 mm³), shafts through pulleys (3), and belt runs tangent to pulleys (4 × 70–122 mm³). Those are deliberate. Any twelfth would be a fault.

Three Fusion traps, recorded so they cost nobody else a morning

  • addExistingComponent(component, matrix) applies the matrix relative to the previous occurrence of that component. A list of placements silently accumulates — the second part lands at the sum of both positions. Add at identity, set transform2 afterwards.
  • snapshots.add() reverts the transform of some referenced occurrences. It did it to every NEMA 17 while leaving the pulleys alone, three times, with no error. The fix is to wrap the part in a native component and move the wrapper.
  • A sketch on xZConstructionPlane has its second axis along −Z. A hole placed at +z lands at −z and cuts nothing, leaving an unhealthy "No target body!" feature behind.

All three produce wrong geometry without raising an error, which is the worst kind. Verified by reading world positions back from root.allOccurrences after every placement — never trusting that a call did what it said.

  • The bed is the slowest axis and nothing has been done about it. It is the only structural gain left worth having.
  • No printed part has been designed. The brackets in the model are plain plates standing in for parts that still have to be drawn.
  • Belt clamps, bed levelling hardware, wiring and drag chain.

Glossary

Back-EMF
Voltage a spinning motor generates against its own supply. Rises with speed; when it meets the supply voltage, the motor stops making torque.
Bedslinger
A printer where the bed moves back and forth for the Y axis, rather than the head. Cheap, and what you drew.
Bowden
Filament drive mounted on the frame, pushing through a tube to the nozzle. Light head, vague retraction.
Direct drive
Filament drive mounted on the moving head. Heavier, but crisp retraction.
Duplicate mode
Both IDEX heads printing the same part simultaneously — two copies in one print time.
GT2
The standard toothed belt profile for printers, 2 mm between teeth.
IDEX
Independent Dual EXtruder. Two heads on one beam, each moving separately.
Input shaping
Firmware splitting a sharp move into two timed nudges that cancel each other's ringing.
Klipper
Printer firmware that runs on a small Linux computer. Does the maths for input shaping; the reason high speeds are achievable at all.
MGN12
A 12 mm steel linear rail with a ball-bearing block riding on it. Far more rigid than plastic wheels.
NEMA 17 / 23
Motor mounting standards — a 42 mm or 57 mm square faceplate. Says nothing about power.
Natural frequency
The rate a structure wobbles at when disturbed. Higher is better. Set by stiffness ÷ mass.
Newton-metre (Nm)
Unit of twisting force. Roughly: holding a 100 g apple on the end of a 1 m stick.
Resonance / ringing
The visible echo after a sharp corner, caused by the head still wobbling.
Second moment of area (I)
How a beam's metal is distributed about its bending axis, in cm⁴. Bigger means stiffer. Material far from the centre counts hugely more.
Springs in series
Springs in a chain. The floppiest one dominates the result.
Stiffness (k)
Force needed per unit of deflection, in N/m. Bigger means more rigid.
TMC2209
A common, cheap, quiet stepper driver chip. Comfortable up to about 2 A.
T-slot / V-slot
Two extrusion groove shapes. V-slot's angled groove also acts as a wheel track; T-slot doesn't. With linear rails you don't need either, so it's about reusability.
Workings — torque at a GT2 20T pitch radius of 6.366 mm, rotor inertia 54 g·cm², 90% drivetrain efficiency · resonance from belt stiffness ≈ 400 kN/m in series with bracket stiffness of 25 / 60 / 150 kN/m for printed, ribbed and aluminium, and with frame stiffness from 48EI/L³ (beam) and 3EI/L³ (upright) at E = 69 GPa · acceleration ceiling ≈ f² × 0.022 · back-EMF at Ke ≈ 0.35 V/(rad/s) · model validated against a measured Ender 3 at ~32 Hz.
